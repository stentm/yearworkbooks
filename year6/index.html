Hyphens for Clarity

A hyphen can completely change a sentence's meaning. Classic examples: a man-eating shark (a shark that eats people) vs a man eating shark (a man who is eating shark meat); re-cover a chair (put a new cover on it) vs recover from an illness (get better); twenty-odd people (about twenty) vs twenty odd socks (twenty mismatched socks).

The Passive Voice

In the passive voice, the subject receives the action instead of doing it: "The cake was eaten by Tom" instead of "Tom ate the cake."

The Subjunctive Form

The subjunctive is a formal verb form used for wishes, suggestions, and things that aren't (yet) true, e.g. "If I were you..." and "It is vital that she attend..."

Semi-colons, Colons & Dashes

Use a semicolon to join two closely related main clauses; use a colon to introduce a list or explanation; use a pair of dashes — like this — to add extra information.

Synonyms & Antonyms

A synonym means almost the same as a word; an antonym means the opposite.

Cohesion Across Paragraphs

Good writers link their ideas across sentences and paragraphs using adverbials of time, place or number ("Later," "Nearby," "Firstly,") and by using pronouns to avoid repeating names.

Fiction Comprehension

The Kite Race

Ravi had built his kite from bamboo canes and an old bedsheet, painstakingly stitching each seam by candlelight for a fortnight. His younger sister, Priya, thought it looked more like a patchwork quilt than a kite, but she kept her opinion to herself — mostly. On the morning of the village kite festival, a stiff breeze rolled in from the hills, and Ravi's hands trembled as he unwound the string.

The other competitors' kites were sleek and brightly coloured, darting confidently into the sky, while Ravi's patchwork creation wobbled and dipped, threatening to nosedive into the crowd. Just as he was about to give up hope, a sudden gust caught beneath the fabric and lifted it high above the others, spinning gracefully against the clouds.

The crowd gasped, then cheered. Ravi's ungainly kite, stitched with such care, soared higher than any other that day. Priya grinned and squeezed his arm. "I take it back," she whispered. "It's the best kite I've ever seen."

Non-fiction Comprehension

The Water Cycle

Water is constantly on the move, travelling endlessly between the oceans, the atmosphere and the land in a process called the water cycle. It begins with evaporation, when heat from the sun turns water in rivers, lakes and seas into invisible water vapour, which rises into the sky. As this vapour climbs higher, it cools and condenses into tiny droplets, clustering together to form clouds. Eventually, these droplets grow too heavy to stay airborne, and they fall back to Earth as precipitation — rain, snow, sleet or hail. Some of this water soaks into the ground and becomes groundwater, while the rest flows across the surface as run-off, collecting in streams and rivers that carry it back to the sea. The entire cycle then begins again, and has done so for billions of years, without ever needing a single drop of extra water.
